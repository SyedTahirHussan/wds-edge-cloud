Checking water-network sensor data at the station and in the cloud

flowmine validates and repairs pressure, flow, level and chlorine readings where they are measured, stores them in an OGC SensorThings server, combines them centrally with laboratory, maintenance and weather data, and drives an EPANET model with the result. It also measures what each side catches and what each costs.

Network
EPANET Net3, 15 stations, 25 sensors
Data
8 simulated weeks at 5-minute steps
Experiments
10, each over 5 random seeds

Browse the code See the results

I built flowmine to work through the three phases of the i3WaterS DC11 doctoral project at the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya (distributed and centralized data mining for water distribution systems in the context of IoT and big data) before discussing them with the supervisors. Nothing here has run at a utility or on a microcontroller. All data are simulated, and the edge code was tested under the MicroPython Unix port on a laptop.

How a reading travels

  1. Station

    Range, rate, flatline and forecast-residual tests, plus a mass balance where a tank has both level and flow. A double-seasonal Holt-Winters forecaster fills short gaps. Readings wait in a local SQLite store when the link is down.

    MicroPython-compatible, no numpy

  2. Uplink

    A 12-byte binary frame per reading, or fewer bytes with send-on-delta, where the cloud runs the same forecaster and the station only transmits when it disagrees.

    Sized for the LoRaWAN EU868 51-byte limit

  3. SensorThings

    A gateway decodes frames into Observations on FROST-Server. The validation flag rides in resultQuality and the model version in the parameters.

    OGC SensorThings API v1.1, MQTT

  4. Central analytics

    PCA and ridge models across sensors, a CUSUM of online chlorine against late laboratory samples, the maintenance log to explain operations, and triage of sensor faults against real network events.

    Telecontrol, lab, maintenance, weather

  5. EPANET twin

    Validated boundary readings drive EPANET one hydraulic step at a time. Simulated values and residuals go back to SensorThings as their own Datastreams.

    EPANET toolkit via WNTR

  6. Model registry

    Models trained centrally, locally or by federated averaging are versioned and sent back to the stations, which can roll back.

    Closes the loop to the station

What the experiments show

Means over 5 seeds, scored on the eighth week, which no model or threshold saw. Whiskers and ± values are one standard deviation across seeds. Each chart can show its numbers as a table.

The station catches fast faults; slow ones need the centre

From its own history a station finds most spikes and stuck readings. Bias and drift look normal to it, and only cross-sensor models and lab samples expose them. Combining the two finds the most faults overall, at a price in false alarms.

Short gaps are a time-series problem, long gaps a spatial one

For a missing pressure reading, the station's own forecast is about as good as anything for the first hour. Beyond a few hours, estimating it from neighbouring sensors wins. Linear interpolation needs the reading after the gap, so a station cannot use it in real time, and it is the worst over a day.

Send-on-delta cuts the radio traffic by about two thirds

Bytes per station per day under four uplink policies. The same records as SensorThings JSON would be 34 times larger than the binary frames.

Validated inputs cut the twin's excess error by more than half

Pressure error of the EPANET twin at nodes with no sensor. Clean inputs still leave an error, because the model has one demand multiplier for the whole network.

During an outage, only the station knows

Link outages of 6 and 24 hours. With validation at the station, more than half of the fault events in a 24-hour outage are flagged while the link is down, and the readings wait in the station's store. A design that validates centrally learns nothing until the backlog arrives.

No training scheme wins at both horizons

Forecast error at a station with no history of its own. Pooled central training gives the best next-step forecast and by far the worst day-ahead one. Federated averaging sits between the two. Whether it pays off between utilities that cannot share data is a question a single simulated network cannot answer.

Phase 1 in v0.2: validation at the station

Version 0.2 adds three things to the station, all fitted or calibrated in the cloud on weeks 1 to 7 and shipped in the model package: split conformal thresholds for the forecast-residual test, a CUSUM on the standardised residuals for slow drift, and a model that predicts each sensor from the other sensor at the same station. Same 5 seeds, same test week as above.

The v0.2 station finds some biases and drifts, and still misses most

The cross-sensor model is what moves bias and drift recall. At a pump station flow and discharge pressure are tied by the pump; at a chlorine logger pressure says little about chlorine, and the model's error there is about as large as the injected bias. The drift CUSUM adds false alarms without adding detections on these seeds. The ablation table in results/summary.md separates the three parts.

Conformal thresholds keep the false-alarm rate near the level asked for

Share of clean test-week samples rejected by the residual test, against the level the thresholds were calibrated for on week 7. One table per sensor stays close to the identity line. Splitting the day into four buckets leaves too few calibration samples per bucket for the 15-minute sensors, so their thresholds become infinite and the test goes quiet.

Reconstruction intervals cover less than they promise

Each reconstructed value gets an interval calibrated on week-7 gaps for 90 % coverage. On test-week gaps the intervals cover less, because samples within a gap are correlated and a week holds few gaps of each length.

The v0.2 station fits easily in an ESP32's memory

Live heap of the station code under the MicroPython Unix port on a laptop, after one day of samples (v0.2 with its cross-sensor weights stored as int8), against the 520 KB of SRAM in Espressif's ESP32 datasheet (version 5.3). This compares two numbers; nothing here ran on an ESP32, and MicroPython there gets only part of that SRAM.

Phase 2 in v0.3: the hydraulic model as a diagnostic tool

Version 0.3 uses the twin's output three ways: it ranks junctions as leak candidates from the pressure residuals, it checks each pressure sensor against the model, and it fits the chlorine decay coefficient to laboratory results. Same 5 seeds; everything is scored on the test week.

With clean data the leak's node is usually near the top of the list; with faulty sensors it often is not

For each of the 92 junctions, the twin's own network model gives the pressure change that a 10 L/s leak there would cause at the 12 pressure sensors, hour by hour. During a leak, the mean residual over the window 6–12 h after onset is compared with each of these signatures, and the junctions are ranked by the angle between the two vectors, the variant that did best on week-7 test leaks. Each seed has 21 leaks: the testbed's own, and 20 placed one at a time at random junctions in three sizes.

  • Leak
  • Candidates 1 to 5 (larger is ranked higher)
  • Pressure sensor

Switch the input to compare. Validation barely closes the gap to the clean input: the faults it leaves in the data (most biases and drifts, see Phase 1) and the meters' noise stay in the residual vector. Which of the two costs more was not separated.

The twin flags pressure faults with far fewer false alarms than the spatial model

The twin, driven by the validated tank levels and flows, predicts every pressure sensor. Its residual, minus the median residual over all pressure sensors at that time, is checked against a split conformal band set on week-7 clean samples. Removing that shared part matters: a faulty boundary meter moves every residual together, and without the correction the bands were several metres wide (the per-variant thresholds are in results/summary.md). A leak moves the residual as well, so this check alone cannot tell a leak from a sensor fault. Recall below is over the pressure sensors only.

A fitted decay coefficient beats the true one in a model without wall decay

Net3.inp ships with no reaction coefficients. The testbed's water loses chlorine through bulk decay (kb = per day) and wall decay; the model here has bulk decay only. Each day kb is refitted to the last 14 days of laboratory results reported by then, 24 to 48 hours after sampling. The fit settles near per day because it has to carry the wall decay too. On week 8 the fitted value gives a smaller error at the sampling points than the true kb in the same model; only the model with both true coefficients does better. Adding the analysers to the fit changes little.

Phase 3 in v0.4: one model over both sides, and what each architecture costs

Version 0.4 adds a stacked detector: one logistic model that reads every score the station and the centre produce for a sample, with its threshold set for a false-alarm budget on week 7. The tables below compare it with the earlier designs. Same 5 seeds, scored on week 8. The resilience matrix planned for this phase was not built.

The stacked model sits on the same front as hybrid v0.1, and its 1 % budget does not hold on week 8

Each line sweeps one method's own score on week 8 and plots the share of faulty samples it flags against the share of clean samples it flags. Rule rejections, cross-checks and missing samples stay flagged at every threshold, which is why the station lines start to the right of the axis. Rings mark the operating point each method actually uses. The stacked model's threshold was chosen so that 1 % of clean week-7 samples were flagged; on week 8 it flags of them.

The centre adds recall to the station's flags; the station's flags did not help the centre's spatial model

Each row holds everything else fixed and switches one exchange on. Edge-validated input lowers the twin's error at unmonitored nodes, but the spatial model scores slightly worse on it than on raw readings and leak ranking does not change. In the other direction, fusing the centre's spatial score with the v0.1 station's flags raises bias recall and raises the false-alarm rate with it, and a model trained in the cloud helps a new station's next-step forecast and hurts its day-ahead forecast.

What each architecture costs and buys

Detection columns are E1 and E11 over all sensors; uplink is the validated policy for designs that validate at the station and the raw policy otherwise (E3); twin error is E6; the outage column is E4's 24 h link outage; RAM is the largest station's live heap under the MicroPython Unix port, not a measurement on an ESP32.

Limits

  • Everything is simulated on one small benchmark network. The maintenance log is synthetic and the chlorine model is first-order.
  • Thresholds are calibrated on a labelled week. A utility has few such labels.
  • The edge code has not run on an ESP32. Timings come from CPython and the MicroPython Unix port.
  • The hybrid's false-alarm rate is about seven times the station's, and it varies widely between seeds.
  • About half of the sensor faults are still raised as network events by the triage.
  • The leak tests add synthetic leaks to the measurements by superposition, so the stations' own reaction to those leaks is not simulated. The twin's single demand multiplier limits localisation even with clean data.
  • The chlorine model is bulk decay only, run on the twin's demand model, with simulated laboratory noise and no temperature dependence.
  • No authentication or TLS on MQTT or HTTP.

The full list is in docs/limitations.md.
